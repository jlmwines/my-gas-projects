## TITLE
Wine by the Sea: The Coastal Plain


## BODY

Where the modern Israeli wine industry was born, on a warm strip of coast cooled by the sea, and some of its oldest vines are still in the ground to prove it.

**At a Glance**
Where: the coastal strip along the Mediterranean, from the Zichron Ya'acov-Hanadiv Valley down through the Judean Coast, mostly 0-150m above sea level.
Climate: warm and humid, tempered by cool breezes off the Mediterranean.
Soil: coastal sands and loams, shaped by the sea's proximity as much as by the ground itself.


**The Story**

Long before anyone talked about Israeli wine regions, the coast was wine country on a serious scale. Its location made it so. This is where the ports were, and wine made close to a port could travel the whole Mediterranean. The largest ancient wine presses found anywhere in the region turned up along this strip, including a genuinely huge winery complex at Yavneh, around 1,500 years old and built to produce wine in quantities meant for export. Even the famous "Gaza wine" grown in the Negev's desert vineyards took its name from a coastal port, the place where it was loaded onto ships.

Some of that ancient wine has found its way back. One of the old local grape varieties, since named Yael, was identified near Ashkelon, and it is grown for commercial wine again today. It's a quiet thread connecting the coast's ancient trade in wine to its modern one.

In biblical times the coast was mostly borderland, Phoenician in the north and Philistine in the south, with the tribes of Asher and Manasseh brushing its edges. Unlike the hill regions, its ancient story is less about tribes and more about trade.

The modern chapter starts in the 1880s, when Baron Edmond de Rothschild set out to restart the local wine industry almost from scratch, and he chose the coast to do it. His investment centered on Zichron Ya'acov, on the southern slopes of the Carmel range, and by 1892 Carmel had built its cellars there. Those cellars are still in use today, making this the oldest continuously producing modern wine region in the country.

That founding choice still shapes the coast. Because the industry took root here first, and because vineyards, once planted, tend to stay planted, many of Israel's older vineyards are still along this strip. A lot of them are Carignan, a grape that arrived early and never left, giving the Coastal Plain some of the oldest vines in the country. Today the region is known less for volume and more for those old vines.


**Why the Coast Grows Differently**

Low altitude and humidity aren't usually a grower's first choice, and today the Coastal Plain isn't considered Israel's most ideal wine terrain for exactly that reason. Low, warm ground ripens grapes quickly, and humid air makes the vineyard work harder to keep the vines healthy. Compare the Negev, where the dry desert air keeps most vine diseases away almost on its own.

But the sea doesn't just bring humidity. It also tempers the heat. Cool Mediterranean breezes move steadily across the vineyards, taking the edge off what would otherwise be a much hotter growing season. In the Galilee, the Golan, or the Judean Hills, altitude does the cooling. On the coast there is no altitude to speak of, so the sea does that job instead.

The other half of the story is learning what to plant. Once studies showed which grapes were less suited to the coast's combination of warmth and humidity, growers shifted their planting accordingly. Heat-tolerant varieties took over: Carignan, already there from the region's early decades, along with Syrah, Marselan, and others chosen specifically to handle the coast's conditions without losing quality. It's a region that adapted to what its own climate actually wants, rather than fighting it.


**What to Expect in the Glass**

Old-vine Carignan is the Coastal Plain's real signature. Vines that have been in the ground this long give smaller crops with more depth and concentration, and Carignan, a grape that thrives in warm Mediterranean climates, shows real character here. Alongside it, the heat-adapted plantings like Syrah and Marselan bring riper, warmer-climate character: generous fruit and fuller body, with a softer line of freshness than you'd find in a wine from the Galilee or the Golan.

As with any region where many producers make their own choices, the best approach is to taste across a few of them. Start with an old-vine Carignan, then try a Syrah or Marselan from the same stretch of coast, and let the region show you its range. It's a style shaped honestly by its own conditions rather than working against them.
